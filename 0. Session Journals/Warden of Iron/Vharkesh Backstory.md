Ash-Tongued Bone-Bound

  

Strong and Resolute, this Kholo, Vharkesh, is covered in ash grey fur streaked with patterns of black and red. Old scars trace faint patterns across his chest and forearms. He stands tall and confident, a khopesh strapped to his back. His eyes burn a soft cerulean, giving an air of serenity unbecoming of an otherwise barbaric looking creature.

—--

  

In the ash-stained grasslands of the Dividing Plains, beneath a crescent moon and the long shadow of the Stormcrest Mountains, there once lived a small family pack of Kholo—a dozen souls bound by blood, by tradition, and by reverent hunger. They were a small part of the greater Dustpaw clan: gnolls by birth, but stewards of memory by practice. Among them was born a runt, grey-furred and long-limbed, whose cerulean eyes caught the starlight like still water. They called him Vharkesh, son of Rhazhka, blood of Tholgrin, shadowed by his older brother Maarnok, who taught him the sacred words spoken before the bone-fire and the prayers whispered over marrow.

The Dustpaw were no strangers to hardship, but they endured. Not through savagery, but through ritual, through balance, and through the cycle of death and consumption. When one fell, their flesh was not wasted. It was consumed in rite, not out of hunger, but remembrance. The Dustpaw believed that in death, a body was not a shell to be discarded, but a vessel still brimming with wisdom, waiting to be passed on.

So it was that the outsiders eventually came.

A band of robed travelers arrived, claiming curiosity, feigning respect. They offered aid, bartered spellcraft, and asked eager questions about the Dustpaw’s customs. The Kholo, cautious but proud, spoke of their rites. Of communion through flesh. Of learning through blood. Of the deep, gnawing echoes of the past.

Vharkesh, barely of age, watched as his elders welcomed the strangers.

He watched them die.

The travelers were not scholars but necromancers. Seekers of godhood in service to the Whispered One, that dread patron of undeath and entropy. They saw in the Dustpaw’s customs not sacred truth, but potential; rituals to be bent and twisted into power. And so after some time was spent gaining the trust of the Dustpaws, they lit their circle of fire, marked the ground with glyphs, and began their work.

It was not resurrection they achieved.

It was annihilation.

When Vharkesh and two others, Krevra, his cunning cousin, and Zhuvaan, the wary uncle, returned from foraging, they found smoke, silence, and the stench of scorched fur. Their kin had been torn open and laid bare in a parody of sacred rite. The bones had not been cleaned. The blood had not cooled. The ritual was a failure, its perpetrators long since fled.

Tears soaked his muzzle as Vharkesh took the first bite.

Not out of madness. Out of duty.

He devoured Maarnok, his brother, whose skull he cleaned and kept. He honored Tholgrin, his father, who taught him to fear waste above death. He spoke the names of the fallen with every swallow, weeping, trembling, but finishing what had been begun. And when it was over, he whispered, “We will not be forgotten.”

He carries them still. In satchels bound to his chest. In beads of bone woven through his braids. In the low voice that murmurs from Maarnok’s skull when the fire burns low and the stars hide their faces.

They are never gone. They are never quiet.

In time, the survivors parted. Zhuvaan sought out the larger Dustpaw tribes in Turst Fields. Krevra vanished into the wilds. But Vharkesh did not return. He had seen what reverence twisted by ignorance could become. His people, for all their discipline, had no defense against the seductive lies of those who cloaked blasphemy in curiosity.

He would not forsake the old ways. But he would rise beyond them.

So he wandered.

—--

It was Maarnok who told him.

Not in speech, not aloud. But in the stillness between footfalls. In the silence between breaths. When the wind died and the forest held its breath, the bones whispered.

Not all memories fade when the flesh is gone. Vharkesh had long known this. His brother had always been the most curious of them—drawn to strange glyphs in the dirt, fascinated by the stories of the elves beyond the treeline. It was Maarnok who once led a foraging party too far south, who saw something then that the others didn’t understand. A shimmer in the air. A woman cloaked in dusklight, who turned the trees inside out and vanished into green.

He never spoke of it aloud while he lived. But in death, the memory surfaced.

And Vharkesh listened.

He followed the path his brother's skull urged him to take. South, into the Verdant Expanse, past the roots of ancient trees and through the memory of long-forgotten fey paths. Wordless dreams guided him. Whispers filled the space between the world and the weave. A name emerged like a bruise beneath the skin: Naerys Moonsigh.

He didn’t know if she was real the first time he saw her.

She stood at the edge of a clearing that should not have existed. Trees perfectly parted, as if the forest itself had chosen to step aside for her presence. No tower. No door. Just a figure cloaked in illusion and silence, eyes like glass and words like knives. Naerys Moonsigh, she called herself. But Vharkesh knew not to trust names. Names could be masks.

He did not ask to be taught. He had learned what asking earned.

Instead, he waited.

He sat at the boundary of her realm, bones of his kin wrapped tight to his chest, the weight of them a comfort and a judgment. She watched him like a scholar might watch a flame, curious if it would burn bright, or simply die out.

When night fell, he spoke. Not to her, but to them. His father. His brother. He spoke of what they had seen, what had been taken. He spoke in the tongue of ash and memory. Not once did she interrupt.

She tested him after the third day.

A shimmer in the trees. A whisper from a voice he knew was dead. Maarnok, laughing the way he used to, calling out from behind a curtain of silver leaves. Vharkesh ran. Fell. Wept. And eventually rose.

He faced the illusion.

“You wear his voice, but you do not speak as him” Vharkesh snarled, He stood strong. I carry that strength. You will not mock it.”

That night, Naerys finally spoke.

“You are not what they see.”

He met her gaze and did not blink.

“We never have been.”

She became his teacher, though she never used the word. Instruction came through motion, not lecture. Their first lesson was a duel. No rules, no rhythm, just spell, bone, and steel dancing through the underbrush. She summoned illusions that blurred the world. He answered with instinct and ferocity. His khopesh carved through glassy shields, not with grace, but with certainty.

She struck him down, of course. Many times.

But he always stood back up.

Each spell he learned was a clawhold carved into a new way of thinking. Not sacrifice, not hunger, not power stolen from the dead. But magic drawn through precision, calculation, and intention. A language older than blood.

He carved runes now instead of flesh. Spoke incantations instead of chants. But still, he whispered to his bones. They guided his blade. They approved.

He expected her to recoil from him, as others did. She never did. She simply listened, corrected, demanded better.

When he asked her once why she allowed him to stay, she only said:

“Because tenacity is rarer than talent. And you, Ash-Tongue, are relentless.”

She marked him in time. Not with ink, but with power. A sigil drawn on the back of his hand in a circle of woven light, only visible when he willed it so. Her trust. Her legacy.

She never asked him to leave. She never said goodbye. One day, he simply woke before her, packed his bones, and walked into the mists of morning.

“They see a beast,” he whispered to his brother’s skull.  
“Let them.”  
“We will show them what beasts can become.”

---

Now he walks with purpose, spell and steel intertwined. Each incantation, a prayer. Each blade strike, a name remembered. Each breath, a voice that is not just his own.

“We are many,” he says, voice low and guttural. “And though I strike with one hand, a dozen guide it.”

He is Vharkesh Ash-Tongue.

And the dead walk with him still.
